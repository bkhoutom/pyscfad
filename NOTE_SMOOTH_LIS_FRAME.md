# Stochastic triples force noise and the smooth LIS frame

> Current implementation: one full occupied block and one full virtual block.
> The internal Fock projector is the identity. Physical LIS/overlap response remains.


Companion to [the smooth CCSD(T) guide](doc/source/user_guide/smooth_lis_triples.md).

Near-degenerate orbitals can rotate rapidly while the subspace they span moves
smoothly. A deterministic contraction can cancel the large terms generated by
those rotations. A finite stochastic contraction can leave a noisy remainder,
which the orbital derivative amplifies. The smooth LIS construction follows the
subspace and chooses its internal coordinates by projecting reference orbitals
and orthonormalizing them with Cholesky factorization.

The discussion below uses real orbitals and a fixed local selection: LIS ranks
and reference anchors are fixed during differentiation.
The overlap, retained subspaces, Fock matrices and current orbitals respond to
the nuclear coordinates.

## 1. Where the inverse orbital gap enters

First work in an orthonormal coordinate system. For a symmetric Fock matrix,

$$
F u_i=\epsilon_i u_i,\qquad u_i^T u_j=\delta_{ij}.
$$

Differentiate this equation and project onto a different eigenvector:

$$
u_j^T\,du_i=
\frac{u_j^T(dF)u_i}{\epsilon_i-\epsilon_j},\qquad i\ne j.
$$

Thus a small perturbation that couples two close eigenvalues can cause a large
change in their **individual eigenvectors**. At exact degeneracy, their
individual directions are not uniquely defined. This is the inverse-gap issue
also described in the [PyTorch `eigh` documentation](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/2.14/generated/torch.linalg.eigh.html).

For a nuclear coordinate R, the pair's rotation rate contains

$$
\frac{u_j^T(\partial F/\partial R)u_i}{\epsilon_i-\epsilon_j}.
$$

This gap is between two occupied orbitals or two virtual orbitals. It is
different from the triples excitation denominator

$$
D_{ijk}^{abc}=
\epsilon_a+\epsilon_b+\epsilon_c-\epsilon_i-\epsilon_j-\epsilon_k.
$$

Two virtual energies can nearly coincide while every relevant D remains
comfortably positive. The difficulty can therefore arise from the choice of
orbital coordinates even when the physical excitation denominators are regular.

## 2. Why deterministic summation cancels the large terms

The complete energy is a scalar. Changing coordinates within the same occupied
or virtual space, while consistently transforming the Fock matrix, amplitudes,
integrals and physical fragment targets, leaves its value unchanged. A smooth
full-Fock formulation makes that coordinate invariance explicit.

In semicanonical coordinates, the derivative distributes the same physical
response among many terms involving orbital rotations. The separate terms can
contain large inverse gaps. Their complete sum has the cancellations needed to
recover the derivative of the invariant scalar.

This does **not** mean that a physical change in F has zero energy derivative.
It means that the arbitrary rapid rotation of the coordinate axes does not
create a singular physical response inside an otherwise regular retained block.

### A two-orbital example

Consider the scalar

$$
E=b^T h(F)b=\sum_i h(\epsilon_i)z_i^2,
\qquad z_i=u_i^T b,
$$

where b is fixed and h is a smooth function on the spectrum. This is an
illustration of the cancellation, not the full triples formula. Matrix
exponentials and resolvents in the triples formulation have the same type of
divided-difference structure.

At the reference point, take a purely off-diagonal perturbation of the 1–2
block in the eigenbasis. Write

$$
\delta=\epsilon_1-\epsilon_2,\qquad
d=u_1^T(dF)u_2,\qquad h_i=h(\epsilon_i).
$$

The two contributions from differentiating z are

$$
dE_1=\frac{2h_1z_1z_2d}{\delta},\qquad
dE_2=-\frac{2h_2z_1z_2d}{\delta}.
$$

Their deterministic sum is

$$
dE_{12}=2z_1z_2d\,
\frac{h(\epsilon_1)-h(\epsilon_2)}{\epsilon_1-\epsilon_2}.
$$

As the eigenvalues approach one another,

$$
\frac{h(\epsilon_1)-h(\epsilon_2)}{\epsilon_1-\epsilon_2}
\longrightarrow h'(\epsilon).
$$

Each separate term can grow like 1/delta, but their sum has a finite limit.
The numerator of the combined expression becomes small at the same rate as
the denominator. Complete deterministic triples contractions enforce analogous
identities among their tensor contributions.

## 3. What finite stochastic sampling changes

Suppose the two terms in that example are estimated by drawing index 1 or 2
with probability 1/2. A draw contributes either `2 h1 z1^2` or `2 h2 z2^2` to
the energy estimate. Its rotation derivative is correspondingly

$$
Y=\begin{cases}
4h_1z_1z_2d/\delta,&\text{probability }1/2,\\
-4h_2z_1z_2d/\delta,&\text{probability }1/2.
\end{cases}
$$

The expectation of Y is the correct deterministic derivative. For the average
of N independent draws, however,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\overline Y)=
\frac{4(z_1z_2d)^2(h_1+h_2)^2}{N\delta^2}.
$$

The **mean** contains the small difference `h1 - h2`; the **noise** contains
`h1 + h2`. The cancellation holds in expectation, but finite sampling need not
realize it accurately. When the remaining factors have nonzero limits, the
standard error grows as `1 / (sqrt(N) * abs(delta))`.

An extreme illustration is `h = 1` and `z1 = z2 = 1` at the reference point.
Every sampled energy equals 2, so the energy sampling variance there is zero.
Yet the sampled derivatives are `+4 d/delta` and `-4 d/delta`. The exact energy
is `b^T b` and its derivative is zero. Accurate sampled energy at one geometry
therefore says little by itself about sampled derivative accuracy.

### Connection to stochastic (T) forces

In our force calculation, the native sampler estimates derivatives with
respect to tensors such as T2 and the transformed integrals. Those derivatives
are then propagated through CCSD response and the orbital construction.

For a collection of intermediate tensors Z, write their nuclear Jacobian as J
and the estimated energy derivative as

$$
\widehat{\overline Z}=\overline Z+\eta,
\qquad \Delta\mathcal F=-J^T\eta.
$$

The force error is the derivative noise multiplied by the same Jacobian used
in deterministic differentiation. If that Jacobian includes eigenvector
rotation factors `1/(epsilon_i - epsilon_j)`, noise in the corresponding
directions can be strongly amplified. For independent sampling with
`Cov(eta) = Sigma/N`,

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(\Delta\mathcal F)=\frac1N J^T\Sigma J.
$$

This explains why stochastic (T) can have a small total energy SE and still
have large force errors. A smaller energy SE target generally buys more
samples, but it does not directly constrain this force covariance.

The precise amplification depends on the sampler and its correlations. A
sampler that enforces the relevant cancellations within each draw could avoid
this particular problem. The `1/(sqrt(N)*gap)` scaling describes an exposed
noise direction; it is not a universal formula for every force component.

### The specific noisy component entering an eigensolver pullback

Let `H = U diag(epsilon) U.T` be the semicanonical Fock eigendecomposition,
and let bar_U be the accumulated cotangent of its eigenvectors, including
amplitude response, integral transformations and target response. Define

$$
A=U^T\bar U,\qquad g_{ij}=A_{ij}-A_{ji}.
$$

Here g is the energy derivative for the infinitesimal rotation with
`K_ij = +1` and `K_ji = -1`. With the full-matrix Frobenius convention for
cotangents, the rotation contribution to the Fock pullback is

$$
(U^T\bar H U)_{ij}^{\mathrm{rotation}}
=\frac{g_{ij}}{2(\epsilon_j-\epsilon_i)},\qquad i\ne j.
$$

For a regular exactly summed energy, the combined numerator g vanishes at
least linearly with the gap. A stochastic error eta_g in that numerator need
not do so. Its contribution is `eta_g / (2 * gap)`.

Noisy amplitude cotangents contribute to this angular derivative, but so do
the other tensor cotangents. For example, under a virtual rotation
`t1 -> t1 R`, the amplitude part for virtual pair a,b is

$$
g_{ab}^{t_1}=\sum_i
\left(t_i^a\bar t_i^b-t_i^b\bar t_i^a\right).
$$

T2 contributes the corresponding contractions on both virtual legs; integral
and target tensors contribute their own contractions. The amplification is
controlled by the error in the **combined angular derivative**, rather than
by the norm of bar_t1 or bar_t2 alone. Section 7 distinguishes this
semicanonical rotation from an intermediate W-basis rotation that already
cancels through re-semicanonicalization.

Deterministic calculations can also suffer from roundoff, incomplete response
terms, or undefined eigenvector derivatives at exact degeneracy. Their
advantage here is the absence of Monte Carlo cancellation error. A genuine
small excitation gap or an unstable retained/discarded boundary remains a
problem for both methods.

## 4. Full-LIS frame used by the implementation

The internal Fock projector is the identity on each complete occupied or virtual
LIS. There are no Fock clusters, projector derivatives, or Fock-dependent frame
rotations. The frame receives no Fock input, hence its Fock cotangent is zero.

At selection, save AO anchors A spanning the retained LIS. At evaluation, form
`X = C C^T S A` from the current LIS C and AO overlap S, and orthonormalize X in S.
This transports the physical retained subspace and overlap response. Metric
normalization still requires a Cholesky factor and triangular solve. These
operations normalize the physical AO metric; they do not fix a Fock eigenframe.

A truncated D/W-selected LIS still has retained/discarded subspace response.
The retained-space derivative avoids internal-eigenvector denominators but must
retain sensitivity across that physical truncation boundary. The identity
Fock projector does not set this physical response to zero.

The current CCSD, MP2 subtraction, and Laplace triples calculations use full
occupied and virtual Fock matrices. Stable matrix-function derivatives include
off-diagonal response without differentiating individual Fock eigenvectors.

## 5. Physical fragment weights and asymmetric triples

Physical S selection is distinct from frame fixing. For occupied-LIS target U,
the energy weight is `M = U U^T`. At Laplace time t, `O = exp(t Foo/2)` gives
`O^-1 M O = L R^T`, where `L = O^-1 U` and `R = O U`. The retained native kernel
contracts directly over S and samples the restricted selected-space terms.
Both target factors and their derivatives are retained. Whole-LIS S uses U=I.

The sampled energy and its tensor derivatives share the same production draws.
Native exponential pullbacks supply the full Fock response. The deterministic
conventional reference checks energy and forces; stochastic canonical sampling
is no longer a supported production path.
